Tick is a small self-hosted app, not a managed service. This page is the honest inventory: what the code does, and what it leaves to you. Nothing here claims an audit.

## What the app does

### Passwords

- Hashed with Node's **scrypt** (`node:crypto`), one random salt per user, stored as `salt:hex`. No plaintext, no reversible encryption, no third-party service.
- Minimum 8 characters, enforced on register, reset and change. There is no complexity rule and no breach-list check.
- Login answers a single generic `401` for both a wrong email and a wrong password, and verifies against a dummy hash when the email is unknown so response time does not reveal whether an account exists.

### Sessions

- Sessions are **sealed, signed cookies** (nuxt-auth-utils), encrypted with `NUXT_SESSION_PASSWORD`. There is no session table, so there is nothing for an attacker to read server-side — and nothing to revoke individually.
- The cookie is `HttpOnly`, `SameSite=Lax` and `Secure` (sent only over HTTPS, or to a loopback address: `localhost`, `127.x`, `[::1]`). `NUXT_SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE=false` removes only `Secure`, and Tick logs a warning at startup when it's off — see [Plain HTTP and the Secure flag](#plain-http-and-the-secure-flag).
- Authorization is never trusted from the cookie. Every authenticated request re-reads the caller's org membership and role from the database: a removed member is locked out on the next request, a demoted one loses privileges immediately.
- Two other cookies exist and neither carries authority: `nuxt-color-mode` (the saved theme) and `tick_tz` (your IANA timezone, e.g. `America/Denver`, so the server renders dates in your zone rather than the container's). Both are readable by page scripts by design, and both are ignored unless they hold a value the server recognises — an unknown timezone falls back to the server's own.
- Revocation works through `users.session_version`: a password **reset** invalidates every session, a password **change** invalidates every *other* session and keeps the current one. Removal from the org also cuts a live session off, on its next request. Short of that, only rotating `NUXT_SESSION_PASSWORD` kills a live session (which logs out everybody).

### Plain HTTP and the Secure flag

Browsers refuse to store a `Secure` cookie that arrives over plain `http://`, with one exception: loopback addresses (`localhost`, `127.x`, `[::1]`). Open Tick at `http://192.168.1.20:3000` and signing in *looks* like it works, but you stay logged out. The sign-in page explains this.

**Setting `NUXT_SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE=false` fixes this, and here is what it costs.** The cookie is encrypted and signed, so nobody can read or forge its contents. It is still a **bearer token**: anyone who copies it *is* that user. Without `Secure`, it travels in cleartext on every request. Anyone who can see the traffic can take it and replay it: someone on the same Wi-Fi, a compromised switch or router, or ARP spoofing on the LAN. It also goes out on any plain `http://` request to that host. Once taken:

- **It never expires.** Tick sessions have no maximum age.
- **Logging out does not revoke it.** Logout only deletes the browser's copy.
- **It dies only** on a password change or reset (`users.session_version`), removal from the org, or rotating `NUXT_SESSION_PASSWORD` (which logs everyone out).

Passwords also cross the network in cleartext at sign-in, whatever this flag says. Plain HTTP exposes the password as well as the cookie.

**Still in force without `Secure`:**

- `HttpOnly`: an XSS can't read the cookie.
- `SameSite=Lax`: other sites can't make authenticated POSTs.
- The CSP and the other response headers.
- The live membership and role check on every request.

None of these help against someone watching the network. Also, without `Secure` a network attacker can more easily plant a session cookie of their own — for example, sign you into *their* account, so your hours land in their workspace.

**Switching schemes on one hostname.** If that host already holds a `Secure` cookie from an earlier HTTPS visit, a later plain-HTTP login (even with `NUXT_SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE=false`) is blocked: the browser won't let an insecure response overwrite a `Secure` cookie, so you stay on the login page. The on-page message covers this case: it tells you to clear the site's cookies if `NUXT_SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE=false` is already set. Logging out over HTTPS first does not fix it — logout leaves an empty `Secure` cookie in place. Clear that site's cookies (or its stored data) in the browser, then log in over HTTP. Avoiding the mix in the first place — one scheme per hostname — avoids this entirely.

**Acceptable:** a network where you trust every device and path. A single-user home LAN, or a VPN or overlay network (WireGuard, Tailscale) that encrypts the traffic itself, count.
**Not acceptable:** any shared, guest or public Wi-Fi, anything reachable from the internet, or a network whose other devices you don't control.

**Prefer, in this order:**

1. **Caddy with `tls internal`.** Real HTTPS on a LAN hostname; trust Caddy's root certificate once per device. See `deploy/Caddyfile.example`.
2. **A VPN or Tailscale with HTTPS certs.**
3. **Your own internal CA with nginx variant A.**

Only then `NUXT_SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE=false`. Accepted values are `true`/`false` (`1`/`0` also work); an empty value or an unrecognized value stops Tick at startup rather than guessing, and Tick logs a warning at boot whenever it's off.

**Behind a TLS reverse proxy, keep `Secure` on (the default).** The browser only ever talks https to the proxy, so the cookie works even though the proxy reaches Tick over http. A cookie without `Secure` would also be sent on the plain-http request that port 80 answers with a redirect, leaking it before the redirect happens. HSTS closes that hole only after the first https visit, or with preload. Also, once a hostname has sent HSTS, browsers force https for it — plain-http access to that name (and to its subdomains, with `includeSubDomains`) stops working anyway, so `Secure=false` gains nothing there. If the same hostname is reachable over both https (Secure cookie) and http, the http side can't replace the existing Secure cookie — see "Switching schemes on one hostname" above for what that looks like and how to recover.

### Tenancy

- Every query is scoped by `org_id`, and entry mutations additionally by `user_id`. Ids are UUIDs and validated as such, so a guessed id from another org returns `404`, not data.
- Role gates live on the server, not just in the UI: only owners and admins can rename the org, manage members or manage invites; only owners can create or grant the `owner` role or manage other owners; the last owner cannot be demoted or removed.

### Public endpoints

- `POST /api/auth/{login,register,forgot,reset}` and `GET /api/invites/lookup/:token` are the only routes reachable without a session.
- `POST /api/auth/forgot` always answers the same `{ok: true}` and pads its response time, so it cannot be used to enumerate accounts. Reset tokens are 36 random bytes, single-use, and expire after one hour.
- Validation errors on those routes are deliberately terse — `{statusMessage: 'Invalid input', data: {fields: […]}}`, field names only — so schema internals never leak.

### Rate limiting

`POST` to each auth endpoint is limited to **10 requests per IP per minute** by default; over the limit the server answers `429` with a `Retry-After` header. Set [`NUXT_AUTH_RATE_LIMIT`](/reference/env#variables) to change the ceiling, or `0` to disable it and let a proxy do the job.

::note
The window is in-memory and keyed on the socket address — not `X-Forwarded-For`, which a client can forge when the app is exposed directly. Behind a reverse proxy every request appears to come from the proxy, so the limit becomes effectively instance-wide. Multi-node deployments should rate-limit at the proxy and set `NUXT_AUTH_RATE_LIMIT=0`.
::

### Response headers

Middleware sets these on every response — pages, assets and API alike:

| Header | Value |
| --- | --- |
| `Content-Security-Policy` | `default-src 'self'`, no external script hosts (only `fonts.googleapis.com` / `fonts.gstatic.com` for fonts), `object-src 'none'`, `base-uri 'self'`, `form-action 'self'`, `frame-ancestors 'none'` |
| `X-Frame-Options` | `DENY` |
| `X-Content-Type-Options` | `nosniff` |
| `Referrer-Policy` | `strict-origin-when-cross-origin` |

`x-powered-by` is stripped. `script-src` does include `'unsafe-inline'` — Nuxt's inline hydration payload requires it — so the CSP is a defense in depth, not a hermetic seal. **HSTS is not set by the app**: it belongs at the TLS-terminating proxy, and both shipped [proxy configs](/installation#reverse-proxy) carry it commented out.

### Input and payload limits

- API request bodies are capped at **1 MB** (`413`); a mutation without a `Content-Length` is refused with `411` so a chunked body cannot slip past the check. `/api/import/*` has its own **5 MB** cap.
- Every body, query and route param goes through zod. `users.theme` accepts known keys only and is capped at 2 KB — a jsonb column is not a dumping ground.
- CSV export escapes per RFC 4180 **and** neutralizes spreadsheet formula injection: a text field starting with `=`, `+`, `-`, `@`, tab or CR is prefixed with an apostrophe, so a maliciously named entry cannot execute in Excel or Sheets.

## What you have to do

::warning
Tick speaks plain HTTP and assumes something in front of it. The list below is not optional for anything reachable beyond localhost.
::

1. **Terminate TLS at a reverse proxy**, and leave `NUXT_SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE` at its default. Use the shipped [`deploy/nginx.conf.example` or `deploy/Caddyfile.example`](/installation#reverse-proxy). Plain HTTP beyond localhost needs `NUXT_SESSION_COOKIE_SECURE=false`. That puts both passwords and session cookies in cleartext, so only do it on a network you fully trust (see [Plain HTTP and the Secure flag](#plain-http-and-the-secure-flag)).
2. **Add HSTS at the proxy** — but only once the certificate is known-good. Browsers cache it, and a broken cert then locks users out for the whole `max-age`.
3. **Generate a strong `NUXT_SESSION_PASSWORD`** (`openssl rand -base64 36`), keep it out of version control, and keep it stable. It is the key to every session cookie.
4. **Pick a [registration mode](/installation#registration-modes).** The default is `open` — anyone who can reach the host can create a workspace. Public instances should run `invite` or `closed`.
5. **Restrict the database.** Postgres is not exposed to the host by the shipped Compose file; keep it that way, and give the app its own role rather than a superuser. With `NUXT_AUTO_MIGRATE=false` the runtime user needs no DDL rights at all.
6. **Back up Postgres.** The 30-day trash is an undo affordance, not a backup — *Delete forever*, a purge past retention and a dropped volume are all unrecoverable. `pg_dump` on a schedule, restore-tested.
7. **Watch the logs** if you run without SMTP: password-reset links are printed to the server console there, which means anyone who can read your logs can take over an account.
8. **Never run [demo mode](/reference/env#variables) against real data.** It wipes and reseeds the demo org on boot.

## Known gaps

Stated plainly, so nobody is surprised:

- **No email verification.** A registered address is never confirmed.
- **No two-factor auth**, no SSO, no OIDC.
- **No per-session revocation.** You can log out everywhere (password change or reset), not a single device.
- **No audit log.** Who deleted what is not recorded beyond the trash's `deleted_at`.
- **No field-level encryption.** Everything is readable by anyone with database access.
- **Rate limiting is single-node and in-memory** — see the note above.
- **Sessions don't expire; logout doesn't revoke a copied cookie.** Only a password change or reset, removal from the org, or rotating `NUXT_SESSION_PASSWORD` kills one.
